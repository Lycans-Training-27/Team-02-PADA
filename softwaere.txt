\section{Hardware Requirements}

\subsection{Electrical and Avionics Design}

The electrical architecture of the PADA aircraft is designed to deliver regulated,
reliable power to high-draw propulsion components and sensitive digital avionics
while maintaining strict weight and safety margins.

\subsubsection{Component Selection and Power Budget}

A comprehensive power analysis was conducted to size the power source and ensure
system stability across nominal cruise and peak throttle flight regimes while
meeting a strict structural weight budget.

\textbf{Battery Endurance Calculation:}
Assuming a continuous nominal cruise draw of $9.28\,\text{A}$ on an $850\,\text{mAh}$
battery, total nominal operational flight time $t_{\text{flight}}$ is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    t_{\text{flight}} = \frac{0.85\,\text{Ah} \times 0.80 \text{ (usable capacity)}}{9.28\,\text{A}} \times 60\,\text{min/hr} \approx 4.40\,\text{minutes}
\end{equation}
This comfortably satisfies the $3\text{--}5$ minute mission profile with a $20\%$
capacity safety buffer while keeping avionics mass well inside the target budget.
The battery energy content ($11.1\text{V} \times 0.85\text{Ah} \approx 9.4\,\text{Wh}$)
is well within the project's $100\,\text{Wh}$ battery energy limit, and a LiPo
(not LiHV) chemistry was selected in compliance with Section~3's constraints.

\subsubsection{Wiring, Connectors, Protection, and Electrical Safety}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-Current Path:} Primary power from the 3S LiPo to the ESC
    utilizes 16 AWG silicone wire rated for up to $30\,\text{A}$ continuous,
    terminated with a lightweight XT30 anti-spark connector serving as the
    master physical arming switch.
    \item \textbf{Low-Voltage Signal Path:} Avionics and servo communication
    lines utilize 26 AWG twisted-pair wiring to reduce high-frequency
    electromagnetic interference (EMI) generated by motor switching.
    \item \textbf{Voltage Regulation \& Protection:} Power to the Matek flight
    controller, sensors, and micro servos is stepped down to $5.0\,\text{V DC}$
    using the 30A ESC's integrated Battery Eliminator Circuit (BEC). An inline
    capacitor bank on the power distribution pads suppresses inductive voltage
    spikes during sudden throttle changes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Component Placement and Integration}

To maintain dynamic balance and aerodynamic stability, hardware components are
arranged within the internal fuselage cavity as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Center of Gravity (CG) Alignment:} The primary mass---the 3S
    LiPo battery ($85\,\text{g}$) and Matek FC ($10\,\text{g}$)---is positioned
    strategically over the calculated aircraft CG ($25\text{--}30\%$ mean
    aerodynamic chord) on an adjustable tray to allow fine pitch trimming.
    \item \textbf{Vibration Isolation:} The flight controller is mounted on
    vibration-damping foam tape to isolate the onboard IMU from high-frequency
    motor vibrations and maintain clean sensor fusion performance.
    \item \textbf{EMI Separation:} The Matek M10Q GPS/compass module is mounted
    externally on an elevated $10\,\text{cm}$ carbon mast clear of motor power
    leads and receiver antennas to prevent electromagnetic compass offsets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Software Requirements}
% ============================================================

\subsection{MAVROS Installation \& SITL Connection}

MAVROS was installed following the official ROS~2 Jazzy package repository and
connected to a running ArduPilot SITL (ArduPlane) instance over UDP. The
connection was verified by monitoring the \texttt{/mavros/state} topic before
proceeding to higher-level node development; a \texttt{connected: true} response
confirmed a stable MAVLink link between MAVROS and the SITL instance.

\subsection{Autonomous Mission Adaptation via Visual Perception}

\subsubsection{Physical System Setup \& SITL Architecture}

The system combines physical camera hardware, software perception, high-level
decision-making, and Software-in-the-Loop (SITL) flight simulation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical Optical Sensing:} A smartphone camera, connected via
    the IP Webcam application over a shared Wi-Fi network, serves as the live
    camera feed. The phone streams video over HTTP, which is captured on the
    companion workstation using OpenCV's \texttt{VideoCapture} interface.

    \item \textbf{Companion Workstation:} A laptop running Ubuntu 24.04 with
    ROS~2 Jazzy processes the live video feed. A dedicated ROS~2 node
    (\texttt{camera\_publisher\_node}) captures frames from the phone stream
    and publishes them on the \texttt{/camera/image\_raw} topic using the
    \texttt{sensor\_msgs/Image} message type via \texttt{cv\_bridge}.

    \item \textbf{ROS~2 / MAVROS Middleware:} A second node
    (\texttt{vision\_processor\_node}) subscribes to the image stream,
    performs the perception and decision logic, and communicates with MAVROS
    through its service interface (\texttt{/mavros/set\_mode}) to convert
    high-level decisions into MAVLink commands.

    \item \textbf{Flight Execution \& Telemetry:} The MAVLink commands are
    received by a simulated ArduPlane instance running inside ArduPilot SITL.
    Flight mode changes and telemetry are monitored live in QGroundControl (QGC).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textit{Note on Flight Control:} No low-level control loops (servo control,
stabilization, or speed holding) were implemented; ArduPilot handles all
low-level flight control. The system's scope is strictly high-level: vision,
decision-making, and mission-mode navigation.

\subsubsection{Unified Mission Scenario \& Perception Requirements}

Rather than writing separate, unrelated scripts for obstacle avoidance and
command detection, a single unified pipeline handles two real-time situations
during one continuous mission flight:

\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{3}
    \item \textbf{Environmental Hazard / Obstacle Avoidance:} The aircraft
    executes a baseline AUTO waypoint mission. When the camera detects a
    red-colored object (simulating an environmental hazard), the system
    switches the flight mode to \texttt{GUIDED}, halting the waypoint mission
    to avoid the hazard. Once the hazard clears from the camera's view, the
    system automatically reverts the flight mode to \texttt{AUTO}, resuming
    the original mission from the current waypoint.

    \item \textbf{Visual Mission Instruction (Flight-Mode Command):} When the
    camera detects a blue-colored visual target, the autonomy layer interprets
    this as an explicit flight command and switches the flight mode to
    \texttt{RTL} via the \texttt{/mavros/set\_mode} service call.
\end{enumerate}

used by the system.

\subsubsection{System Architecture \& ROS 2 Interface Design}

The software architecture follows a modular, decoupled two-node design pattern,
matching the logical flow required by the mission:

\begin{center}
[Visual Perception] $\rightarrow$ [State Interpretation] $\rightarrow$
[Autonomy / Decision Engine] $\rightarrow$ [ROS 2 / MAVROS Gateway] $\rightarrow$
[ArduPilot SITL / QGC]
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\texttt{camera\_publisher\_node} (Publisher):} Interfaces
    with the physical smartphone camera via IP Webcam, capturing frames at
    approximately 30~FPS, resizing them to $640\times480$, and publishing them
    as \texttt{sensor\_msgs/Image} messages over \texttt{/camera/image\_raw}.

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{PUBLISH.png}
        \caption{\texttt{camera\_publisher\_node} publishing camera image frames.}
        \label{fig:publish}
    \end{figure}

    \item \textbf{\texttt{vision\_processor\_node} (Subscriber \& Service
    Client):} Subscribes to the image topic, processes frames using HSV color
    thresholding to detect hazards and visual targets, and calls the MAVROS
    \texttt{/mavros/set\_mode} service (\texttt{mavros\_msgs/SetMode}) to
    request flight mode changes.

    \begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{SUBSCRIBE.png}
        \caption{\texttt{vision\_processor\_node} subscribing to image topics and processing frames.}
        \label{fig:subscribe}
    \end{figure}
\end{itemize}

\centering
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Topic/Service} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\hline
\texttt{/camera/image\_raw} & \texttt{sensor\_msgs/Image} & Live camera feed \\
\hline
\texttt{/mavros/set\_mode} & \texttt{mavros\_msgs/SetMode} & Flight mode change requests \\

\noindent
This two-node design keeps sensing and decision-making decoupled: the publisher
node is solely responsible for reliable camera acquisition, while the processor
node focuses entirely on perception and decision logic. This separation
simplifies debugging, since each node's behavior can be verified independently
by inspecting its published topic.

\noindent
\textbf{Error Handling:} If a frame fails to be captured
(\texttt{cap.read()} returns \texttt{False}), \texttt{camera\_publisher\_node}
skips the cycle and retries on the next timer callback without crashing the
node. \texttt{vision\_processor\_node} wraps its frame-processing logic in a
\texttt{try/except} block and uses a non-blocking service wait
(\texttt{wait\_for\_service(timeout\_sec=0.5)}) so that a transient decoding
error or unresponsive MAVROS service never freezes the subscription loop.

\subsubsection{Engineering Justifications \& Tradeoff Defense}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Perception Strategy:} HSV color space was selected over RGB
    because it decouples color information (Hue) from lighting intensity
    (Value and Saturation), ensuring robustness against variable ambient
    lighting, shadows, and camera shake in dynamic environments.

    \item \textbf{Detection Reliability \& Filtering:} To eliminate false
    positives and visual noise from triggering unintended flight mode changes,
    a strict pixel-area threshold (\texttt{threshold\_area = 600 pixels}) was
    enforced. A state transition only occurs when the detected color mask's
    non-zero pixel count exceeds this threshold, preventing small color
    artifacts or noise from being misread as a valid hazard or target.

    \item \textbf{Visual Command Design:} Distinct, high-contrast colors (red
    for hazards/evasion, blue for operational targets) were assigned to
    specific flight modes (\texttt{GUIDED}, \texttt{RTL}, \texttt{AUTO}). The
    hazard and target logic are mutually exclusive: the target-detection check
    is only evaluated when no hazard is currently active, preventing
    conflicting simultaneous commands.

    \item \textbf{Obstacle Assessment \& Path Planning:} At the current
    high-level scope of the project (per the assignment's note that no
    low-level control loops are required), obstacle response is implemented
    as an immediate mode switch to \texttt{GUIDED} upon hazard detection,
    which halts the AUTO waypoint mission and hands control to the operator/
    autonomy layer. Once the hazard clears, the system autonomously reverts
    to \texttt{AUTO}, resuming waypoint navigation from the current mission
    leg without requiring mission re-upload.

    \item \textbf{Computational Efficiency:} By setting the video capture
    buffer size to 1 (\texttt{CAP\_PROP\_BUFFERSIZE = 1}) and resizing frames
    to $640\times480$, the vision pipeline consistently operates at
    approximately 30~FPS with minimal CPU footprint, ensuring real-time
    responsiveness on standard workstation hardware.
\end{itemize}
